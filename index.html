Phishing is a con that arrives as a message

An attacker pretends to be someone you trust, such as your bank, a colleague, a delivery company or IT, and tries to get you to click, log in, pay or share something you shouldn't.

Most

breaches involve a human element such as a clicked link, a stolen password or a believable lie, according to Verizon's yearly breach reports.

1 click

is all it takes to hand over a password or install malware on a work laptop.

Anyone

can be targeted. Attackers pick the person who has access, not the person who has the biggest title.

What the attacker wants

Your credentials

A fake login page captures your username, password and sometimes your MFA code.

Your device

An attachment or download installs ransomware or a remote-access tool.

Your money

A fake invoice, gift card request or changed bank detail redirects a payment.

Your information

Personal data, customer lists or internal documents for later fraud.

What you will do in this module. Inspect a fake email, judge six web addresses, learn the psychological tricks attackers use, read six real incidents, build your own defence checklist, then take a 10 question quiz.

Tap a highlighted part

Every dashed element in the email is clickable. Some are red flags, and all of them are worth a look.

    The 30 second email check. Sender address, tone and deadline, greeting, link destination, attachment, request. Check the real address behind a link by hovering over it, or by long-pressing on a phone.

    Fake websites and look-alike links

    A fake page can copy a logo perfectly. The address bar is harder to fake, so read it carefully.

    How to read a web address

    https://login.paypal.com.secure-verify.net/signin

    The part that tells you who owns the site is the domain just before the first single slash, read from the right, ending at the last two or three pieces. Everything to the left of it is only a label that the owner can choose freely. Here the real owner is secure-verify.net, no matter how many familiar names sit in front.

    Look-alike spelling

    arnazon.com uses "rn" to look like "m". micros0ft swaps a letter for a zero.

    Padded subdomains

    Brand names placed on the left of an unrelated domain.

    Odd symbols

    An @ in a link hides the real destination after it.

    The padlock myth

    HTTPS only means the connection is encrypted. Phishing sites use it too.

    The same con on different channels

    Email

    Phishing

    Mass messages posing as a brand or service.

    Email

    Spear phishing

    Written for one person using details from LinkedIn, your website or earlier leaks.

    Email

    Whaling and BEC

    Impersonates executives or suppliers to move money. Often has no link at all.

    SMS

    Smishing

    Texts about parcels, tolls, bank alerts or refunds.

    Phone

    Vishing

    A caller claiming to be IT, the bank or a government office. Voice cloning is making these more convincing.

    QR code

    Quishing

    A QR code on a poster, parking meter or email that leads to a fake login page.

    Chat

    Collaboration apps

    A "colleague" on Teams, Slack or WhatsApp asking for a quick favour.

    MFA

    Prompt bombing

    Dozens of approval requests until you tap Approve just to make them stop.

    Pause. Check. Verify. Report.

    Four moves cover nearly every attack. Tick the habits you commit to adopting.

    1

    Pause

    Urgency is the attacker's tool. Slow down before you click, pay or reply.

    2

    Check

    Sender address, link destination, attachment type, and whether the request is normal.

    3

    Verify

    Confirm through a second channel you already trust, never the contact details in the message.

    4

    Report

    Use the report button or forward it to your security team. Early reports protect colleagues.

    0 of 8 habits chosen.

    If you already clicked

    1. Do not enter anything else. Close the page.
    2. Tell IT or security straight away. Speed matters more than embarrassment, and nobody is punished for reporting.
    3. If you typed a password, change it right away, on every site where you reused it.
    4. If you opened an attachment, disconnect from the network and wait for IT instructions.
